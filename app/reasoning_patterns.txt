• ABDUCTIVE REASONING:
◦ Using the best explanation you can find to understand why something happened, like a detective figuring out who committed a crime based on clues.

• ANALOGICAL REASONING:
◦ Understanding something new by comparing it to something familiar, like learning how a cell works by comparing it to a tiny factory.

• BAYESIAN REASONING:
◦ Updating what you believe as you get new information, like changing your mind about tomorrow's weather when you see dark clouds forming.

• CAUSAL REASONING:
Understanding how one thing causes another, like knowing that pushing a domino will make all the other dominoes fall.

• COUNTERFACTUAL REASONING:
◦ Thinking about what might have happened if things were different, like imagining how your day would have gone if you'd woken up earlier.

• CRITICAL REASONING:
◦ Carefully examining information to decide if it makes sense, like fact-checking a news story before believing it.

• DEDUCTIVE REASONING:
◦ Starting with general rules and figuring out specific cases, like knowing a dog will have four legs because all dogs have four legs.

• EMOTIONAL REASONING:
◦ Making decisions based on how you feel, like choosing what to eat based on what food makes you happy.

• FORMAL REASONING:
◦ Following strict rules of logic, like solving a math problem using specific steps and formulas.

• HEURISTIC REASONING:
Taking mental shortcuts to solve problems quickly even if the solution isn't perfect, like guessing how many jellybeans are in a jar by comparing it to a similar jar you saw before.

• INDUCTIVE REASONING:
◦ Making general rules from specific examples, like deciding all dogs are friendly after meeting several friendly dogs.

• INFORMAL REASONING:
Using everyday logic and common sense to solve problems, like figuring out what to wear based on the weather.

• INTUITIVE REASONING:
◦ Making decisions based on gut feelings without knowing exactly why, like instantly knowing something isn't right.

• LEGAL REASONING:
◦ Applying laws and rules to specific situations, like a judge deciding if someone broke a law.

• LOGICAL REASONING:
◦ Following a clear chain of if-then statements to reach a conclusion, like knowing that if all birds have wings, and a penguin is a bird, then a penguin must have wings.
